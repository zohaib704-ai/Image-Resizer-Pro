How to compress an image under 100 KB (without ruining it)

Every web form, job portal, and email attachment seems to cap uploads at 100 KB. Here's how to hit that target every time.

Jan 12, 2026 6 min read By the editors

Why 100 KB is such a common ceiling

Government forms, university applications, and job portals still enforce 100 KB uploads because it keeps their storage and bandwidth costs predictable. The number is arbitrary, but the constraint is real — and most phone photos these days come in at 3–8 MB.

The good news: getting under 100 KB is almost always achievable with two levers — quality and dimensions. Pull both, and you keep an image that still looks sharp on screen.

The two levers you actually control

  • Quality (compression strength). Lower quality discards more visual detail. For photos, 65–80% is usually invisible to the eye. Below 50%, artefacts appear around edges.
  • Dimensions (pixel count). A 4000 × 3000 photo has 12 million pixels. A 1200 × 900 photo has 1.08 million. That's a 91% reduction before compression even starts.

For most uploads, reducing dimensions is the bigger win. A 1200-pixel-wide photo at 75% quality typically lands between 60–120 KB depending on subject matter.

Step-by-step (in your browser)

  1. Open the ImageResizer Pro homepage and upload your image.
  2. Click Resize and enter a width between 1000 and 1200 pixels. Leave the aspect ratio locked.
  3. Click Apply Resize.
  4. Click Compress, choose JPEG as the format, and drag the quality slider to 75%.
  5. Download. Check the file size shown in the tool panel. If it's still over 100 KB, drop the quality to 65% and try again.

Two iterations is usually enough. The tool tells you the exact output size each time, so you're never guessing.

What about PNG and WebP?

PNG is lossless — meaning it doesn't discard detail, so it stays large. A photo compressed as PNG often lands at 2–3× the JPEG size. Don't use PNG for photos. Use it only for graphics with flat colours or transparency (logos, icons, screenshots with text).

WebP compresses better than JPEG at the same visual quality — often 25–35% smaller. If the receiving system accepts it, WebP is the smartest choice. Most modern browsers do.

The trick most tools don't tell you

If your image has a solid background — a scanned document, a product photo on white — convert to JPEG with a white fill first. Many compressors try to preserve transparency, which bloats the file for no reason. ImageResizer Pro already fills white when you export to JPEG, so this is handled automatically.

What "too compressed" looks like

Look for blocky patches in smooth areas (sky, skin), halos around sharp edges, and muddy colour gradients. If you see those, back off the quality slider by 10% and try again. A 110 KB file that looks clean beats a 95 KB file that looks like a JPEG from 2004.
