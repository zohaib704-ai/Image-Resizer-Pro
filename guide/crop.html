How to crop a photo

Drag the handles, lock to a ratio, and commit — every social and print ratio covered.

Jan 12, 2026 4 min read By the editors

What this tool does

The Crop tool overlays a draggable selection box on your image. Drag the corners to resize the selection, drag inside to move it, and pick a ratio preset from the dropdown if you want a fixed shape. When you click Apply Crop, everything outside the selection is discarded and the remaining region becomes the new image.

When to use it

  • Resizing for a specific platform (1:1 Instagram, 16:9 YouTube, 9:16 stories).
  • Trimming unwanted edges — a hand in the corner, a messy background.
  • Framing a subject more tightly for impact.
  • Removing borders or margins from a scan before OCR.

Step-by-step

  1. Upload your image and open the Crop tool.
  2. The selection box appears pre-sized. Drag its corners to adjust.
  3. To lock the shape, pick a ratio from the dropdown: 1:1 (square), 4:5 (portrait), 4:3 (classic), 16:9 (widescreen), 9:16 (story), or leave on Free.
  4. Drag inside the selection to reposition it.
  5. Click Apply Crop to commit.

Tips for best results

  • Enhance first. Enhancing after cropping can't see the pixels you discarded. Fix the whole image first, then crop.
  • Leave 2–3 mm of margin. Cropping exactly to the subject's edge looks cramped. A tiny breathing room makes photos feel more professional.
  • Lock the ratio before dragging. If you drag first, then lock, the selection snaps to the nearest matching shape — you may lose the framing you wanted.
  • For documents, crop tight. The opposite of photos: for OCR or print, trim to the exact page edge.

Common mistakes

  • Cropping too tight. The subject's head touches the edge — always leave room to breathe.
  • No ratio lock for social posts. You end up with a 1.3:1 image when you needed 1:1. Lock first.
  • Cropping before rotating. A sideways photo cropped "upright" produces a sideways crop. Rotate first, then crop.
  • Cropping after compression. Compression introduces artefacts at edges. Crop first, then compress — not the other way around.
